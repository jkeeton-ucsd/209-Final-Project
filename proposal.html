NYPD Complaints Explore Proposal

DSC 209R · Final project proposal

Where Complaints Pile Up, They May Not Stick: NYPD Misconduct Allegations Across New York City

Team
  • Aidan Sanchez
  • Anirudh Yetikuri
  • Jack Keeton
Dataset
Civilian Complaints Against New York City Police Officers (ProPublica, from NYC Civilian Complaint Review Board records). Each row is one allegation against an officer received between 2000 and 2019.

Supporting data: NYPD precinct boundaries (NYC Open Data), and median household income and population from the 2020–2024 American Community Survey (B19001, B01003; tracts placed in precincts using the Census Gazetteer).

What we intend to do

We will build an interactive article that lets readers explore civilian complaints against the New York City Police in three parts.
Where do complaints come from? An animated map displays the precincts of New York City, colored by the number of complaints received per resident. We will also have supplemental interactions to help readers locate a point in time, a neighborhood, or a complaint type to dig deeper.
What happens after a complaint is filed? We will show how often the review board substantiates, exonerates, or can't decide each complaint. We will highlight the differences in substantiation between precincts.
Why might outcomes depend on where you live? We will show how complaint volume and substantiation rates relate to neighborhood income and population, and let readers explore the data by precinct. Due to this being an observational dataset, we will be careful not to imply causation, but we will provide relevant reporting to help readers question why precincts may behave differently.

What will be the most challenging part of this project to design, and why?

The most challenging part of the project to design will be the development of an interactive tool showing where complaints come from. After initial exploration of the dataset, preliminary prototypes showed that the chart appears to be static over the entire timeline of the dataset. We need to add interactivity that allows the reader to pinpoint a specific time, a neighborhood, or specific complaint type. To combat the static appearance of the chart, adding a per resident complaint filter to ensure we aren't just looking at population differences in precincts and specific windows with filter-ability of the complaint type will help bring both more interactivity and more dynamic appearance to the chart. Making these filters work together without leaving precincts with too little data to show, or overwhelm the reader with controls will prove to be difficult.
