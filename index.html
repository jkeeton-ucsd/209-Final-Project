NYPD civilian complaints, 2000–2019

Where complaints pile up, they're less likely to stick

Between 2000 and 2019, New Yorkers filed — complaints with the Civilian Complaint Review Board (CCRB) against the officers in this dataset. They weren't spread evenly. — of them came from just 10 of the city's 77 precincts.

This page looks at three things: where complaints come from, what happened once they were filed, and why the outcome might depend on where you live.

2 · What happened next

Most allegations are never substantiated, and force is the hardest to prove

For each allegation the CCRB reaches one of three findings. Substantiated: the officer did it and it was misconduct. Exonerated: it happened but was within NYPD rules. Unsubstantiated: there wasn't enough evidence to decide.

What we can't see yet: policing intensity

Precincts that get more stops and searches have more encounters that end in disputed accounts, often with no witnesses or video. The CCRB can't substantiate those. Testing this means joining per-precinct stop and arrest counts, which this dataset doesn't include.

Takeaway

The neighborhoods that complain the most get the least resolution

Complaints are concentrated in a few predominantly Black and Latino precincts. Allegations from those precincts are — less likely to be substantiated than allegations from the city's quietest precincts. The type of allegation doesn't explain the gap. Income follows the same pattern: A New Yorker's chance of having a complaint upheld seems to depend partly on where they live.

About the data
  • Source: ProPublica's release of NYC CCRB records (Sept 1985 – Jan 2020). Precinct boundaries come from NYC Open Data. This page uses complaints received from 2000 to 2019, because earlier years are sparse.
  • This is not every complaint. The release only covers officers who were still on the force in 2020 and had at least one substantiated allegation. That pushes the substantiation rates here above the CCRB's true overall rate, so compare precincts with each other, not with outside figures.
  • Units: a complaint can include several allegations against several officers. Act 1 counts complaints. Acts 2 and 3 count allegations, since the CCRB rules on each one separately.
  • Location and time: the data only records the precinct and the month received, so Act 1 adds up complaints by precinct and month.
  • Income and population come from the Census Bureau's American Community Survey, 2020–2024 5-year estimates (tables B19001 and B01003). Census tracts don't line up with precincts, so each tract is assigned to the precinct that contains its center point. Income bracket counts are summed per precinct and the median is estimated from the combined distribution, top-coded at $200k. These figures postdate the complaints (2000–2019), and some neighborhoods have gentrified since, so treat income as a rough, present-day picture.
  • Act 1 and Act 2 use raw counts. "Per resident" figures in Act 3 divide by the precinct's residential population, which understates how many people pass through commercial areas like Midtown. Precincts with fewer than 100 allegations are grayed out in Act 2 and left out of Act 3, because their rates are too noisy.
