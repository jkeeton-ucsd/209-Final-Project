NYPD Complaints Story Proposal

NYPD civilian complaints, 2000-2019

What happens when you report police misconduct in New York City?

The answer may depend heavily on where you call home.

By Aidan Sanchez, Anirudh Yetikuri and Jack Keeton

Final project, DSC 209R · Data: ProPublica, NYC Civilian Complaint Review Board

Any individual who believes a New York City police officer abused their authority, used excessive force, or treated them with discourtesy can file a complaint with the Civilian Complaint Review Board, an independent civilian oversight body that reviews each allegation.

Between 2000 and 2019, ProPublica collected — complaints on officers still on the force in 2020. Complaints aren't evenly distributed across the city and neither is the board's response. Where people complain most, often in lower-income neighborhoods, their complaints are least likely to be upheld.

What happens next

Every complaint gets reviewed. Not every neighborhood gets the same results.

For each allegation, the board reaches one of three conclusions. Substantiated means the officer committed misconduct. Exonerated means the officer was found not guilty. Unsubstantiated means there wasn't enough evidence to decide.

If the process worked the same everywhere, the share of complaints upheld would look about the same from one precinct to the next. Before you see the results, make a guess.

What we can't see: other possible factors

The level of policing, the crime rates in the area, local laws and interventions, and other factors not in this dataset could affect the true substantiation rate. We cannot claim causality without a more comprehensive dataset.

What it means

Your address could shape whether oversight works for you

Complaints concentrate in a handful of neighborhoods, and those are the places where complaints are least likely to be upheld. Allegations from the busiest precincts are — less likely to be substantiated than those from the quietest. Income follows the same pattern.

So the answer to the question we started with may depend, in part, on where you call home.

About the data

  • Source: ProPublica's release of NYC CCRB records (Sept 1985 - Jan 2020). Precinct boundaries come from NYC Open Data. This story uses complaints received from 2000 to 2019, because earlier years are sparse.
  • This is not every complaint. The release only covers officers who were still on the force in 2020 and had at least one substantiated allegation. That pushes the substantiation rates here above the board's true overall rate, so compare precincts with each other, not with outside figures.
  • Allegation types: the CCRB files every allegation under one of four categories: force, abuse of authority, discourtesy, or offensive language. Those categories and the specific allegations within them (physical force, stop, frisk, and so on) come straight from the source data. The one grouping we added: offensive language is combined with discourtesy in the filters and type comparisons, because it is a small share of allegations (about 2%). Abuse of authority spans dozens of specific allegations (stops, searches, frisks, entering premises…), so as a category it outnumbers force, even though physical force is the most common single allegation in most precincts.
  • Units: a complaint can include several allegations against several officers. The first section counts complaints; the later sections count allegations, since the board rules on each one separately.
  • Location and time: the data only records the precinct and the month received, so complaints are added up by precinct and month.
  • Income and population come from the Census Bureau's American Community Survey, 2020-2024 5-year estimates (tables B19001 and B01003). Census tracts don't line up with precincts, so each tract is assigned to the precinct that contains its center point. The median is estimated from combined income brackets, top-coded at $200k. These figures postdate the complaints, and some neighborhoods have gentrified since, so treat income as a rough, present-day picture.
  • Per resident figures divide by the precinct's residential population, which understates how many people pass through commercial areas like Midtown. Precincts with fewer than 100 allegations are grayed out or left out of substantiation-rate views, because their rates are too noisy.
