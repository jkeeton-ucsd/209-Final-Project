# "Where You Report Matters": 2-minute video script

About 290 spoken words, which is about 1:55 at a relaxed pace. Every number below was checked against the page's data model.
Story arc: **and** (Act 1: complaints concentrate) → **but** (Act 2: those places get fewer upheld) → **therefore** (Act 3 + takeaway).

## Setup before recording

- **Slides:** open `video/slides.html` in Chrome and press **F** for full screen. Use → / Space to advance. The bars animate on entry, so start recording first. Add `?still` to the URL if you'd rather save PNGs and drop them into the editor.
- **Live page:** record the **published** site, not localhost:
  `https://jkeeton-ucsd.github.io/209-Final-Project/?demo&theme=light`
  - Set the browser window to 1920×1080, with zoom at 100–110% and the bookmarks bar hidden.
  - `?demo` clears any saved neighborhood (so you can pick it on camera) and speeds up Act 1 playback to about 15 s.
  - **Keys:**
    - **1–6** glide to each scene: 1 title · 2 map · 3 guess · 4 scatter · 5 mix · 6 takeaway
    - **S** spotlights the current scene
    - **P** plays/pauses the map
    - **H** hides the cursor
    - **?** lists the keys
  - **Reload before each take.** The guess can only be made once per page load.
- **Narration:** record it separately on a headset mic, then cut the screen recording to fit.

### 0:00–0:05 · Title card *(slide 1)*
> "Where You Report Matters, by Aidan Sanchez, Anirudh Yetikuri and Jack Keeton."

### 0:05–0:17 · Hook *(slide 2: the bars grow)*
> "If a New York police officer mistreats you, you can file a complaint with an independent review board. In Chinatown, 40% of allegations get upheld. In East New York, which files nearly four times as many complaints per person, it's 19%."

### 0:17–0:27 · Who it's for *(live page, key 1)*
**Do:** while speaking, pick **Brownsville** in "Where do you call home?"
> "Our story is for any New Yorker who might file one. The records exist only as tables of officers. We put them on a map, and you follow your own neighborhood. I'll pick Brownsville."

### 0:27–0:47 · Act 1: *and* complaints concentrate *(key 2, then P)*
**Do:** let the map play for about 10 s, then click a line in the trend chart to pin it.
> "First, where complaints come from. We count per resident, so big precincts can't rank high on size alone. The timeline marks moments like the 2011 stop-and-frisk peak. The top ten neighborhoods, with Brownsville first, file over three times as many complaints per person as the rest of the city."

### 0:47–1:07 · Act 2: *but* they get fewer upheld *(key 3)*
**Do:** click **Brownsville** (dark, outlined in berry) as the guess, and let the 3-step transition run. Then hover one precinct so the side panel fills.
> "But what happens to those complaints? Our favorite part: before showing you, we ask you to guess where complaints are upheld most. Most people pick a dark one, like I just did. Now the same map recolors by upheld rate. Half of the ten busiest neighborhoods turn red, below the city average. Of the ten quietest, only one does."

### 1:07–1:32 · Act 3: *therefore*, ruling things out *(key 4, then key 5)*
**Do:** on the scatter, click **Per resident**, then **Median income**. Press 5 for the mix chart.
> "Across every precinct, more complaints means a lower upheld rate: a correlation of minus 0.5. It holds per resident, and it tracks income. Maybe busy precincts just file harder-to-prove complaints? This chart tests that. Precincts upheld least and most file almost the same mix of complaints, which predicts about 25% for both. Complaint type doesn't explain the gap."

### 1:32–1:55 · Takeaway *(slide 3; optional 2-second cut to key 6 for Brownsville's personal sentence)*
> "So allegations from the busiest quarter of precincts are 27% less likely to be upheld. The places that lean on oversight most get the least out of it. Watching the same map recolor makes that hard to miss, and following your own neighborhood makes it personal. Where do you call home?"

## What changed from the draft, and why

- **Midwood → East New York in the hook.** Midwood (18.5%) ranks only 28th of 73 for complaints per resident, so it doesn't show the "busy places get less" point. East New York is 4th, with 18.8% upheld.
- **Bed-Stuy → Brownsville as the demo neighborhood.** Bed-Stuy is upheld at 25.7%, *above* the 24.8% city average, so it turns blue in Act 2 and works against the story. Brownsville is #1 per resident, upheld at 22.1%, and turns red.
- **"The busiest flip below average" → "half of the busiest vs. one of the quietest".** Only 5 of the 10 busiest are below average (the page itself says so). The contrast with the quietest ten is the honest version, and it's still striking.
- **Mix chart narration** now matches its default view, which groups precincts by upheld rate (lowest quarter 20%, highest 34%, both expected near 25%).
- **Removed "we have shown it disproportionately affects low-resource neighborhoods".** The income link is weaker than the volume link, and the page itself says it can't establish causes. The takeaway now says *why the visualization works* (the same map recolors, plus personalization), as the rubric asks.
- **Cut about 190 words.** The draft ran about 480 words, which is over 3 minutes spoken.
